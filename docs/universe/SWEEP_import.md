2026-10-06. Builds on `docs/universe/SWEEP_legal.md` (US extra-label pass) and `docs/LYMPHOMA_UNIVERSE.md` section K.
Status markers: **VERIFIED** = operative text read on the primary regulator/legislation site and quoted;
**SECONDARY** = read on a reliable non-primary site; **INFERRED** = my reasoning from verified text, not stated by the regulator.

## 2. UK — the Special Import route. VERIFIED, and it is NOT the cascade limb I expected

### 2.1 The cascade itself does NOT cover a foreign HUMAN medicine
`Veterinary Medicines Regulations 2013` (S.I. 2013/2033), **Schedule 4 paragraph 1(2)** (E+W+S version, as amended to 17.5.2024 by
S.I. 2024/567), read verbatim from legislation.gov.uk:

> "(2) If there is no authorised veterinary medicinal product in the United Kingdom for a condition the veterinary surgeon
> responsible for the animal may, in particular to avoid unacceptable suffering, treat the animal concerned with the following
> ("the cascade"), cascaded in the following order—
> (a) a veterinary medicinal product authorised in the United Kingdom for use with another animal species, or for another
> condition in the same species; or
> (b) if there is no such product that is suitable, either—
>   (i) a human medicinal product **authorised in the United Kingdom**; or
>   (ii) a **veterinary** medicinal product not authorised in the United Kingdom but authorised in another country for use with
>   any animal species ...; or
> (c) if there is no such product that is suitable, a veterinary medicinal product prepared extemporaneously by a pharmacist, a
> veterinary surgeon or a person holding a manufacturing authorisation..."
>
> "(3) In the case of a veterinary medicinal product imported from another country, if the veterinary surgeon has not obtained a
> certificate from the Secretary of State under regulation 25(5) permitting importation, the veterinary surgeon must obtain a
> certificate from the Secretary of State before administration."

URL: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2013/2033/schedule/4
(the word "country" at (b)(ii) and (3) replaced "member State" on 31.12.2020 by S.I. 2019/676 reg 3(34)(a)(i) as amended by
S.I. 2020/1461 — i.e. post-Brexit GB law expressly extends the limb to THIRD countries.)

**So limb (b)(ii) is no help for Starasid**: Starasid is a *human* medicine in Japan, and limb (b)(i) requires a human medicine
authorised *in the United Kingdom*. VERIFIED.

### 2.2 The provision that DOES cover it: Schedule 4 paragraph 7, "Treatment in exceptional circumstances"
Verbatim (E+W+S):

> "**7.**—(1) Where the health situation so requires, and where there is no suitable veterinary medicinal product available either
> as an authorised product or under the cascade, a veterinary surgeon may treat an animal with **a medicinal product authorised in
> another country**; but a veterinary surgeon who has not obtained a certificate from the Secretary of State under regulation 25(5)
> permitting importation must obtain a certificate from the Secretary of State before treating the animal.
> (2) The certificate may be granted subject to any condition the Secretary of State thinks fit."

Note the words: **"a medicinal product"**, not "a veterinary medicinal product". This is the limb that reaches a Japanese human
product. It sits *below* the whole cascade ("no suitable product ... either as an authorised product or under the cascade"), so the
vet must first exhaust UK-authorised vet drugs, UK-authorised human drugs (which includes **generic cytarabine injection** — see
§6) and third-country vet products. VERIFIED from the legislation; the "below the cascade" ordering is the statute's own wording.

### 2.3 Import authority
`VMR 2013 reg 25` (E+W+S), verbatim:
> "(1) No person may import or be concerned in the importation of an unauthorised veterinary medicinal product except in accordance
> with this regulation.
> (5) A veterinary surgeon may import an unauthorised veterinary medicinal product that is authorised in another country if it is
> for the purpose of administration by that veterinary surgeon or under that veterinary surgeon's responsibility under the cascade
> **or administration in exceptional circumstances in accordance with Schedule 4**; the import must be in accordance with the
> appropriate certificate granted by the Secretary of State, and the product may be imported by the veterinary surgeon personally
> or by using a wholesale dealer or pharmacist as an agent."

URL: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2013/2033/regulation/25
So: the vet is the importer (or uses a wholesaler/pharmacist as agent), and the Sch 4 para 7 certificate is the import authority.
VERIFIED.

### 2.4 Scheme mechanics — VMD guidance
Current VMD cascade guidance (https://www.gov.uk/guidance/the-cascade-prescribing-unauthorised-medicines) states the GB cascade in
four steps and adds the express exception: **"In exceptional circumstances, a human medicine may be imported from outside of the
UK"** with a Special Import Certificate. It also states the mandatory conditions: the vet must make a clinical assessment, the
animal must be under their care, **"You must obtain the owner's consent"**, records kept **5 years**, and a human medicine may not
be prescribed "simply because it is cheaper" than an authorised alternative. VERIFIED (gov.uk, primary).

Detailed mechanics come from **VMGN No. 5 "Import Certificate Schemes"** — which carries the banner
**"Document Withdrawn as out of date"** (last updated October 2013, pre-Brexit) on the gov.uk asset store, so treat the numbers as
indicative of practice, not current law:
https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6825cb47940c028bcf34e031/VMGN_05_Import_Certificate_Schemes.pdf
- Two certificate types then existed. The one that matches Starasid is the **Special Treatment Certificate (STC)**: para 22 —
  "If a veterinary surgeon considers that there is not a suitable veterinary or human medicinal product authorised in the UK or a
  suitable VMP authorised in another EU MS to treat a condition, a veterinary surgeon may apply for an STC to import a suitable
  authorised product from outside the EU." Para 23 — "If the veterinary surgeon identifies a human medicinal product as being the
  only suitable alternative, this will require an STC, **regardless of whether it is from Europe or a third country**."
- **Who applies:** para 16 — "You may only apply for an SIC or STC if you are a veterinary surgeon registered with the Royal
  College of Veterinary Surgeons (RCVS)." The holder is "normally ... the individual veterinary surgeon who is caring for the
  animal concerned". Products "do not need to pass through a wholesale dealer; veterinary surgeons may act as their own importers"
  (para 17).
- **Evidence required** (para 25): "(a) the clinical details/history of the animal; (b) reference to any alternative products
  authorised in the UK (or EU) and a justification as to why these products cannot be used; (c) previous relevant treatments;
  (d) the results of any diagnostic tests which have been performed." Para 26: "A separate justification should be included on each
  application. It is not sufficient to refer to previous applications."
- **First-time product** (para 36): because Starasid has (probably) never been imported, the VMD additionally requires
  "(a) copy of the Summary of Product Characteristics (SPC) or datasheet **in English**" and "(b) copies of the labels".
  Para 37: VMD may request data direct from the manufacturer. INFERRED that Starasid is a "new" product to the VMD — not checked.
- **One animal per application** (para 40): "An STC application must be supplied for each individual animal."
- **Chronic/long-term use is expressly contemplated** (para 42): "Generally, it is not permitted to hold stocks in veterinary
  practices of any imported human medicines or of veterinary medicines imported from countries outside of the EU. ... **For longer
  term use in a particular animal, an individual STC application naming that animal should be submitted and the quantity applied
  for should correspond to the dosage required by that animal.**"
- **Validity** (paras 14, 32): "the import certificate will remain valid until the quantity specified has been imported and used,
  or the expiry date stated on the certificate, whichever occurs first. The period of validity for the import certificate **does
  not have to extend to the whole course of treatment**." "The certificate will be valid for **no more than one year** and for
  SIC/STC the total amount required and proposed dosage schedule should reflect this." Para 30: repeats are possible online
  ("e.g. if you are permitted repeats at 6 monthly intervals") but **"For an STC ... all initial applications for a particular
  animal must be assessed."**
- **Timescales** (para 52): new product 15 working days (urgent 5); previously imported 10 working days (urgent 2).
- **Fees** (para 53): online SICs free, online repeat STCs free, hard-copy STC £30, hard-copy repeat STC £30.
- **No class restriction on cytotoxics was found.** The only product-class cautions in VMGN 5 are: import certificates are "less
  suited" to food-producing species and to **vaccines** from outside the EU (para 11), and controlled drugs additionally need a Home
  Office import licence (para 48). Cytarabine ocfosfate is not a controlled drug. VERIFIED as an absence in this document; I did
  not find a separate VMD cytotoxic-handling rule (not searched exhaustively).
- Advertising/promotion of specially imported products is unlawful (VMD blog, 13 Sep 2023).

Scheme now runs through a new digital service: applications only via the new service since **15 July 2024**
(https://www.gov.uk/government/news/further-changes-to-our-special-imports-digital-service; portal www.vmdconnect.uk). SECONDARY/
primary-news.

### 2.5 UK bottom line (so far)
There IS a documented lawful route in Great Britain: **VMR 2013 Sch 4 para 7 + reg 25(5)**, operated as a VMD Special Import
Certificate, applied for by the RCVS-registered vet treating the named dog, one application per animal, certificate up to one year,
renewable, and the guidance expressly addresses "longer term use in a particular animal". The gate is that the vet must show no
suitable UK-authorised product — and a UK marketing authorisation for **cytarabine injection** exists, so the justification has to
be about the *route* (oral, at home, daily) rather than the molecule. See §6.

## 4a. US — INAD / investigational exemption, 21 CFR Part 511. VERIFIED verbatim

Full text read from the official CFR XML:
https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2024-title21-vol6/xml/CFR-2024-title21-vol6-sec511-1.xml
(statutory hook: FD&C Act §512(j); the regulation is "New animal drugs for investigational use exempt from section 512(a)").

### What it permits
§511.1(b): "A shipment or other delivery of a new animal drug ... intended for **clinical investigational use in animals** shall be
exempt from section 512(a) and (m) of the act if all the following conditions are met". Conditions, verbatim in the operative parts:
- **(b)(1) label:** "Caution. Contains a new animal drug for use only in investigational animals in clinical trials. Not for use in
  humans. Edible products of investigational animals are not to be used for food unless authorization has been granted by the U.S.
  Food and Drug Administration or by the U.S. Department of Agriculture." (The edible-product machinery at (b)(5) is irrelevant to a dog.)
- **(b)(4) before shipping:** "Prior to shipment of the new animal drug for clinical tests in animals, the sponsor of the
  investigation shall submit in triplicate to FDA a 'Notice of Claimed Investigational Exemption for a New Animal Drug' including a
  signed statement containing the following information: (i) The identity of the new animal drug. (ii) All labeling and other
  pertinent information to be supplied to the investigators... (iii) The name and address of each clinical investigator. (iv) The
  approximate number of animals to be treated (or if not available, the amount of new animal drug to be shipped)."
  (The extra (b)(4)(v) items — withdrawal time, max daily dose, start/end dates — are required only "If the new animal drug is given
  to **food-producing** animals", so not for a dog.)
- **(b)(7) investigator qualification and records**, 2-year retention; **(b)(8)(ii)** sponsor must provide current monitoring and report
  safety findings.
- **(b)(2)/(b)(3):** due diligence that it will "actually be used for tests in animals", and shipment records for 2 years.
- **(b)(10):** a NEPA claim for categorical exclusion (§25.30/§25.33) or an environmental assessment.
- Submissions go to "Center for Veterinary Medicine, 7500 Standish Pl., Rockville, MD 20855" (§511.1(e)).

### The two provisions that decide the pet-dog question
1. **§511.1(b)(8)(iii): "Shall not unduly prolong distribution of the new animal drug for investigational use."**
2. **§511.1(d) Termination of exemption:** the exemption ends if "(2) The continuance of the investigation is unsafe or otherwise
   contrary to the public interest **or the drug is being or has been used for purposes other than bona fide scientific
   investigation**".
Together these mean the Part 511 exemption is for a **bona fide investigation**, not for treating one named pet indefinitely.
A client-owned pet dog CAN lawfully receive the drug — but only as an enrolled subject of a real clinical investigation with a
protocol, a sponsor, a qualified investigator and monitoring. **VERIFIED from the regulation's own text**; the "not for one named pet
indefinitely" reading is **INFERRED** from (b)(8)(iii) + (d)(2), which is as far as the text goes.

### Importation under Part 511 — §511.1(b)(9), verbatim
> "(9) If the shipment or other delivery of the new animal drug is **imported or offered for importation into the United States** for
> clinical investigational use in animals, it shall also meet the following conditions:
> (i) The importer of all such shipments or deliveries is an agent of the foreign exporter residing in the United States or the
> ultimate consignee, which person has, prior to such shipments and deliveries, informed FDA of his intention to import the new animal
> drug as sponsor in compliance with the conditions prescribed in this subdivision; or
> (ii) The new animal drug is shipped directly to a **scientific institution with adequate facilities and qualified personnel to
> conduct laboratory or clinical investigations** and is intended solely for use in such institutions and which institution has
> submitted a statement as sponsor of the investigation."

**This is the route a veterinary teaching hospital would use**: limb (ii) lets Nippon Kayaku ship Starasid directly to the institution,
which is itself the sponsor. VERIFIED. No separate "foreign approval" test applies — Part 511 does not care that the drug is approved
in Japan; it is simply an unapproved new animal drug in the US either way.

### Is there a CVM compassionate/emergency-use mechanism for an individual animal?
**No such mechanism was found in Part 511, and Part 511 contains no analogue of the human expanded-access rules (21 CFR 312.300-320).**
There is also **no "expanded access" or "single-animal IND" regulation in 21 CFR Parts 511-516** that I could locate.
Marked **NOT FOUND**, not "does not exist" — I have not exhausted CVM's Program Policy & Procedures Manual (e.g. PPM 1243.2100,
1243.4000, 1243.4065) which I did not read in full.

### 1.5 Published VETERINARY use of this exact drug (new to the record)

### 1.6 The UC Davis PK study is itself evidence a US import route works
PMID 37670479's authors are at the **William R. Pritchard Veterinary Medical Teaching Hospital and School of Veterinary Medicine,
UC Davis** — a US institution that dosed 4 dogs with oral cytarabine ocfosfate. So the drug *was* lawfully obtained in the US for
research. The dogs were "3 healthy female hound dogs and 1 healthy male Beagle", i.e. **laboratory research animals**, which maps onto
**21 CFR 511.1(a)** (tests "in animals used only for laboratory research purposes"), not the client-owned-animal limb (b).
**INFERRED** — the paper does not state its regulatory route, and I did not find an INAD number.

## 3. EU — Regulation (EU) 2019/6 gives NO route for this product. VERIFIED verbatim

Read from the consolidated text on EUR-Lex (CELEX:32019R0006),
https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32019R0006

**Article 112 — "Use of medicinal products outside the terms of the marketing authorisation in non-food-producing animal species"**,
verbatim:
> "1. By way of derogation from Article 106(1), where there is no authorised veterinary medicinal product in a Member State for an
> indication concerning a non-food-producing animal species, the veterinarian responsible may, under his or her direct personal
> responsibility and in particular to avoid causing unacceptable suffering, exceptionally treat the animals concerned with the
> following medicinal product:
> (a) a veterinary medicinal product authorised under this Regulation in the relevant Member State or in another Member State for use
> in the same species or another animal species for the same indication or for another indication;
> (b) if there is no veterinary medicinal product as referred to in point (a) of this paragraph, **a medicinal product for human use
> authorised in accordance with Directive 2001/83/EC or Regulation (EC) No 726/2004**;
> (c) if there is no medicinal product as referred to in point (a) or (b) of this paragraph, a veterinary medicinal product prepared
> extemporaneously in accordance with the terms of a veterinary prescription.
> 2. Except as regards immunological veterinary medicinal products, where there is no medicinal product available as referred to in
> paragraph 1, the veterinarian responsible may under his or her direct responsibility and in particular to avoid causing
> unacceptable suffering exceptionally treat a non-food-producing animal with **a veterinary medicinal product authorised in a third
> country for the same animal species and same indication**.
> 3. The veterinarian may administer the medicinal product personally or allow another person to do so under the veterinarian's
> responsibility, in accordance with national provisions. ...
> 5. This Article shall apply also when an authorised veterinary medicinal product is not available in the relevant Member State."

**The final limb, Art 112(2), fails Starasid on three independent counts:**
1. it must be a **veterinary** medicinal product — Starasid is a human product;
2. authorised in the third country **for the same animal species** — Starasid's Japanese authorisation is for humans, not dogs;
3. **and the same indication** — Starasid's indications are adult ANLL and MDS, not canine lymphoma.
Art 112(1)(b) is confined to human medicines authorised **in the Union** (Dir 2001/83/EC or Reg 726/2004), which Starasid is not.
**So there is no EU cascade route for cytarabine ocfosfate.** VERIFIED from the Regulation's own text.
(Arts 113 and 114 are the food-producing-terrestrial and aquatic cascades and are quoted in full in my working notes; their
third-country limbs carry the identical "veterinary medicinal product ... same animal species and same indication" wording.)

**The two other EU derogations do not help either**, both read verbatim:
- **Art 110** ("Use of immunological veterinary medicinal products") — paras (2) and (3) let a competent authority allow a product
  "not authorised within the Union", but the whole article is limited to **immunological** products. Not applicable.
- **Art 116** ("Health situation") — "a competent authority may allow the use in its territory of veterinary medicinal products not
  authorised in that Member State, where the situation of animal or public health so requires, **and the marketing of those veterinary
  medicinal products is authorised in another Member State**." Member-state-to-member-state only. Not applicable.

**Member-state named-patient/import schemes:** under 2019/6 the operative decision sits with the national competent authority, and
member states run their own import paperwork for Art 112(2) products. I did **NOT** verify any individual member state's scheme
(e.g. Germany's §73(3) AMG, the Netherlands, Ireland's HPRA). **NOT CHECKED** — and because Art 112(2) itself excludes a human
third-country product, a member-state scheme could at most implement a limb that does not reach Starasid. Any national route would
have to rest on national law outside 2019/6, which I did not search.

**This is a correction to `docs/universe/SWEEP_legal.md`**, whose "Not checked" section says "the prescribing cascade (Regulation (EU)
2019/6 Articles 112-114) has an import limb that may permit a product authorised in a third country, so cytarabine ocfosfate could
have a route there." Verified: **it does not.** The import limb is restricted to veterinary products for the same species and
indication. The UK, having rewritten the cascade post-Brexit, is the jurisdiction with the wider limb (Sch 4 para 7), not the EU.

## 4b. US — importation for research. VERIFIED

FDA's own import page, "Importing — Veterinary Drugs" (https://www.fda.gov/animal-veterinary/import-exports/importing-veterinary-drugs):
> "New animal drug substances for which there are no approved New Animal Drug Applications (NADAs) or Investigational New Animal Drug
> (INAD) exemptions, consigned to veterinarians for use in their private practices, or to manufacturers, or distributors, may not be
> legally imported into the United States."
and it may be imported only where the consignee is "the holder of an approved NADA, ANADA, or a conditional NADA for the imported new
animal drug substance; or the sponsor or investigator named in an INAD exemption for the imported drug substance; or the investigator
conducting tests in vitro and in laboratory research animals". "All unapproved drugs will be detained and refused at the port of entry."

So the **importer's status is the whole test**: named sponsor/investigator on an INAD, or a laboratory-research investigator.
A private-practice vet consignee is expressly excluded. VERIFIED.

## 4c. US — Personal Importation Policy. It does NOT apply to animals. VERIFIED, stated plainly

FDA Regulatory Procedures Manual Chapter 9, subchapter **9-2 "Coverage of Personal Importations"** (March 2024, version 05),
https://www.fda.gov/media/71776/download — read in full. Operative text, §9-2-5:
> "FDA personnel may allow entry of shipments when the quantity and purpose are clearly for personal use, and the product does not
> present an unreasonable risk to the user. ... Although FDA may use discretion to allow admission of certain violative items, **this
> should not be interpreted as a license to individuals to bring in such shipments.**"
and the four-part factor test is written entirely around a human patient:
> "(d) the individual seeking to import the product affirms in writing that it is for **the patient's own use** (generally not more
> than 3 month supply) and provides the name and address of the **doctor licensed in the U.S.** responsible for **his or her**
> treatment with the product, or provides evidence that the product is for the continuation of a treatment begun in a foreign country."
The follow-up advice it tells staff to give is "The patient's physician should consider for example, enrolling the patient in an
Investigational study or applying for Investigation New Drug (IND), Compassionate IND, or Treatment IND exemption" — all CDER/human
mechanisms. **No reference to animals, pets or veterinarians anywhere in 9-2.**

And FDA says so expressly in **Import Alert 66-40** ("Detention Without Physical Examination of Drugs From Firms Which Have Not Met
Drug GMPs"), https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cms_ia/importalert_189.html :
> "Divisions should follow the guidance in the Regulatory Procedures Manual (RPM) Chapter 9-2 for Personal Importation shipments.
> **Note that personal importation is not allowed for unapproved animal drugs.**"

**Plain statement: the Personal Importation Policy is not a route for a pet dog. It is enforcement discretion, not a permission, it is
written for human patients, and FDA has stated in writing that it does not cover unapproved animal drugs.** VERIFIED.

## 4d. US — compounding from bulk, GFI #256. A real route in principle, blocked in practice by the bulk substance

GFI #256, "Compounding Animal Drugs from Bulk Drug Substances" (final, April 2022), https://www.fda.gov/media/132567/download —
read in full. It is **enforcement discretion, not an authorisation**: "Contains Nonbinding Recommendations".

**First, a correction to the premise of the question.** The two published FDA *lists* of bulk drug substances apply to
(i) **office stock** for nonfood animals, and (ii) antidotes for food animals / sedatives for free-ranging wildlife.
They do **not** gate **section A, "Compounding for Nonfood-Producing Animals: Patient-Specific Prescriptions"**, which is the route for
one named dog. So "is it on the list?" is the wrong question for a patient-specific prescription — **there is no list requirement.**
VERIFIED from the structure and text of the guidance.

Section A's conditions, verbatim in the load-bearing parts — FDA "generally does not intend to take enforcement action ... when **all**
of the circumstances below are present":
1. compounded by or under the direct supervision of a veterinarian, or a pharmacist in a State-licensed pharmacy or Federal facility;
2. "compounded in full compliance with State laws and regulations governing drugs, pharmacy, and veterinary medicine";
3. "All bulk drug substances, inactive ingredients, and finished drug products used in compounding meet the standards set in any
   applicable USP-NF monograph and comply with other FD&C Act requirements for drug components" — and footnote 28 spells out the hard
   one: "**under section 502(o) (21 U.S.C. § 352(o)), all bulk drug substances must be made in a facility registered with the FDA,
   otherwise they are misbranded**";
4. dispensed by the pharmacy on a prescription for a specific patient to the prescribing vet or the owner, or by the vet to the owner;
5. not a copy of a marketed FDA-approved or indexed drug — "a drug compounded from bulk drug substance is a copy if it: has the same
   active ingredient or active moiety as a marketed FDA-approved or indexed drug, **and** can be given by the same route of
   administration";
6. "If the compounded animal drug has any of the same active ingredient moiety(ies) as one or more marketed FDA-approved or indexed
   drugs, the compounder has determined and documented the reason(s) why none of these drugs can be used as the source(s) of the active
   ingredients";
7. adverse events reported on Form FDA 1932a within 15 business days;
8. specified labelling, including "This is a compounded drug. Not an FDA approved or indexed drug."

**How cytarabine ocfosfate fares, condition by condition:**
- **Condition 5 (copy): PASSES.** Footnote 29 defines active moiety as excluding "those appended portions of the molecule that cause
  the drug to be an ester, salt ... or other noncovalent derivative". Cytarabine ocfosfate is cytarabine 5'-(sodium octadecyl
  phosphate) — a phosphate **ester** — so its active moiety is **cytarabine**, the same as approved cytarabine injection. But the
  copy test is conjunctive and requires the **same route**: oral vs injection. So it is **not** a copy. (INFERRED application of the
  guidance's own definitions.)
- **Condition 6: the hard one, and probably fatal.** Because the active moiety *is* cytarabine and FDA-approved cytarabine injection
  is marketed, the compounder must document why approved cytarabine cannot be the source of the active ingredient. The honest answer
  is that you cannot make cytarabine ocfosfate from cytarabine by *compounding* — attaching a stearyl phosphate is a chemical
  synthesis, i.e. manufacturing. The guidance's own listed acceptable reason ("The chemical properties of the FDA-approved ... drug
  prevent its practical and effective use in the compounding of a specific drug") arguably covers that, but the conclusion is then
  that what is needed is **synthesis of a new active ingredient**, which is not compounding at all. **INFERRED.**
- **Condition 3: blocking.** The bulk substance must come from an **FDA-registered facility**, and must meet any applicable USP-NF
  monograph. **There is no USP-NF monograph for cytarabine ocfosfate** (it is a JP-only substance), so that clause is vacuous — but
  I found **no FDA-registered bulk source of cytarabine ocfosfate API**. Research-grade material is sold (e.g. MedChemExpress lists
  "Fosteabine") but research-grade is not an FDA-registered bulk drug substance. **NOT FOUND**, so treated as a practical blocker,
  not a verified impossibility.
- **Cytotoxics:** GFI #256 contains **no** cytotoxic- or hazardous-drug-specific condition; the searches for "cytotoxic",
  "antineoplastic" and "hazardous" in the guidance return nothing. USP <800> and State pharmacy law would apply via condition 2.

**Compounding from an APPROVED drug is a different and easier policy** than compounding from bulk — but it is useless here, because
there is no approved oral cytarabine product to reformulate, and you cannot turn approved cytarabine injection into the ocfosfate
prodrug by compounding. (Approved-drug compounding is outside GFI #256's bulk policy.)

**Also noted and not applicable:** GFI #256 footnote 9 records FDA's shortage process, which may include "refraining from taking
action against imports of foreign-approved versions of the drug product" (https://www.fda.gov/animal-veterinary/product-safety-information/animal-drug-shortage-information).
That applies to a shortage of an **FDA-approved animal drug**. There is no approved US cytarabine-ocfosfate animal drug to be in
shortage, so this does not reach Starasid. VERIFIED as inapplicable.

## 4e. US — MUMS and conditional approval. Available in principle, and there is a canine-lymphoma precedent

FDA, "Conditional Approval Explained: A Resource for Veterinarians"
(https://www.fda.gov/animal-veterinary/resources-you/conditional-approval-explained-resource-veterinarians), verbatim:
- "Only animal drugs for minor species or **minor uses in a major species** are eligible for conditional approval" under the MUMS Act
  (2004); Congress added **expanded conditional approval** in 2018 for major-species drugs addressing "a serious or life-threatening
  condition" or "an unmet animal or human health need" where demonstrating effectiveness would require complex studies.
- Evidence standard: "the drug company must show that the drug has a '**reasonable expectation of effectiveness**', but has not yet
  proven that it meets the 'substantial evidence' standard" — while **full safety must still be proven**.
- Duration: "valid for one year", renewable annually "for up to four more years, for a total of five years".
- **"The Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act doesn't allow extra-label ... use of conditionally approved animal drugs."**
Regulations: 21 CFR Part 516 (MUMS designation, indexing); §511.1(g) extends Part 511 to investigational use in support of indexing.
Dogs are a **major** species, so the route is "minor use in a major species" (the threshold FDA applies is small annual case numbers)
or expanded conditional approval. **Precedent exists:** rabacfosadine (Tanovea) was conditionally approved for **canine lymphoma**,
and paclitaxel conditionally approved for canine mammary carcinoma — so FDA has accepted canine lymphoma as within this pathway.
**But this is a sponsor's drug-development programme, not a route to treat one dog**: it needs a sponsor willing to run target-animal
safety and effectiveness work and file an application. For a Japanese product whose MAH is discontinuing it, there is no sponsor.

### Canada — Emergency Drug Release (EDR). The clearest named-animal scheme after the UK
Health Canada, "Health Canada's special access programs: Request a veterinary drug through EDR"
(https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/drugs-health-products/special-access/veterinary-drug.html):
- **Who:** "veterinary practitioners (veterinarians) may request authorization to access unapproved drugs for emergency veterinary use."
- **Eligibility:** "an animal or group [with] serious or life-threatening conditions that can't be treated with conventional therapies.
  These therapies have failed, are not suitable or are not available in Canada."
- **Mechanism:** the vet submits an EDR Request Form plus Fee Form, and must **confirm the manufacturer can supply the drug**;
  Health Canada responds "within 2 business days"; the authorisation letter permits the **manufacturer to sell a stated quantity to the
  named veterinarian**, and "is valid until the quantity of drug identified is depleted or expires."
- **Repeat supply:** each request is separate — there is no standing supply authorisation. The vet must account for all quantities in
  follow-up reporting, and is responsible for "ensuring the decision to prescribe the drug is supported by credible evidence" and for
  monitoring and reporting results and ADRs.
- Volume: the programme runs 600-1,000 veterinary requests a year (Health Canada / published commentary).
- Legal basis: the EDR provisions of the **Food and Drug Regulations** as amended in 2019 to cover the sale of a new drug for emergency
  veterinary treatment (Canada Gazette Part I, Vol 153 No 19, 11 May 2019, "Regulations Amending Certain Regulations Made Under the
  Food and Drugs Act (Sale of a New Drug for Emergency Treatment)"). **SECONDARY** — I read the canada.ca programme page and the
  Gazette title, not the regulation's section text.
- **The decisive practical point:** EDR authorises *a manufacturer to sell*. It therefore depends on Nippon Kayaku agreeing to export
  to a Canadian vet, repeatedly, for years, for a product it is discontinuing. That is a commercial question, not a legal one.

### 6.2 Is the Japanese prodrug necessary, or only more convenient?

**Already answered in the record, and the answer is "not necessary".** `docs/LYMPHOMA_UNIVERSE.md` §K.2 rebuilt all four programs
from lawful agents only, after removing cytarabine ocfosfate and the anti-CD20 antibody, and **all four still close** — the B-cell and
T-cell brains close on hydroxychloroquine + verdinexor + matched-donor transplant + **cytarabine infusion** + 23.4 Gy + repeated
**intrathecal methotrexate**. Ocfosfate is in none of the lawful programs. So this sweep does not reopen a closure; it answers a
jurisdictional question about an agent the model has already done without.

On the pharmacology, three points, each at its own strength:
1. **The approved alternative reaches the compartment harder, not softer.** The record (§G/H.3) has **measured** CSF ara-C of
   **8.3 µM** after IV infusion (PMID 1742843) against a **derived** time-average of **0.3-1.4 µM** on oral ocfosfate (measured
   troughs 0.04-0.27 µM; PMID 37670479). So the US-approved injectable is the *better*-exposed route in the compartment that matters,
   and per rule 14 the measured value in the right compartment outranks the inferred one. **VERIFIED against the repo's own record.**
2. **What the prodrug uniquely buys is continuity from a tablet at home.** Terminal half-life 23.3-29.4 h in the dog on oral CO
   (PMID 37670479), versus ara-C's own short half-life, so once-daily oral gives sustained exposure that an SC bolus does not.
   Subcutaneous and infusional cytarabine are published in dogs (PMIDs 31769013, 22943060, already in `SWEEP_legal.md`) and owners can
   be taught SC dosing, but matching a 1-3 year continuous exposure that way means q12h injections or repeated infusions, each with its
   own burden. **So: a genuine pharmacokinetic convenience, not a unique capability.**
3. **The Japanese label itself discourages the multi-year continuous schedule the model wanted.** The approved schedule is
   intermittent (2-3 weeks on, 2-3 weeks off) and §8.4 warns that "with long-term use adverse reactions appear more strongly and may
   become protracted". That matches the repo's existing grade of **ASSUMED beyond about 2 years** and **CONTRADICTED for continuous
   600 mg/day** (§H.3) — it does not change it.

**Conclusion: the Japanese prodrug is more convenient (oral, at home, once daily), not necessary.** The closure does not depend on it,
and the compartment exposure that drove its selection is exceeded by an FDA-approved, UK-authorised, Health-Canada-authorised
alternative that already has a lawful extra-label route in every jurisdiction examined.
