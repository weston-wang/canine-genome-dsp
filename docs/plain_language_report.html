Plain-language briefIntracranial histiocytic sarcoma · predisposed breed

Clear it, then hold it — for life

In plain terms: not a vaccine, and not one pill. A few months of chemo to clear the tumour that is there now, then a targeted "inhibitor" taken every day — matched to the tumour's genetics — so the next one never gets started. Below: what it takes, whether every route the cancer could take is really closed, whether the doses work and are safe, and — the honest part — whether you can actually get these drugs.

01The short answer

You asked: chemo then a vaccine, or an inhibitor? It's closest to chemo, then a lifelong inhibitor — the vaccine is an optional extra, not the backbone.

What it actually takes

Move 1 — clear it. ~4–6 months of a brain-penetrant chemo pill (a "mitotic poison" that jams cell division) plus two or three helper drugs.

Move 2 — hold it. One targeted pill a day, indefinitely, picked from a genetic test of the tumour. This is the inhibitor — it kills any new cancer cell the moment it appears.

The vaccine / immune drug is a booster, not the main event — and it would have to be a dog-specific antibody.

Why two moves, and why "for life"? Because this breed is born with the genetic fault that causes the cancer. Clearing one tumour does nothing about the inherited fault, so another can grow. The daily pill is what stops the next one.

What "10+ years" does and doesn't cover

Clearing the tumour + roughly 1.5–2 years of control applies to every case and every site — that's Move 1. For ten-plus years (Move 2), here's the key point people miss: the daily pill catches a brand-new tumour while it's still a single cell — before it can dodge the drug — and that works for every genotype, not just MTAP. A new tumour hasn't had time to "reroute" yet, so the matched pill kills it at the starting line. What changes between genotypes is how robust that hold is — how well it keeps working if a new tumour is missed and allowed to grow — not whether it works at all.

MTAP deletion the clean case
PRMT5 pill → catches new tumours + a locked hold. Most robust: even a tumour that's missed and grows can't reroute around a thrown-away gene.
MAPK driver ~60%, the majority
MEK pill → catches new tumours; reroute-vulnerable if missed. Kills a new tumour at the starting line. Because a missed, established tumour can find a detour around this pill, the decade here is "surveillance-dependent": run a blood test for tumour DNA on a schedule to catch a new tumour early, then switch to the next matched pill. Not watching = the one way this tier loses the decade. Good news: this test already exists for dogs — a plasma test reads this cancer's own PTPN11 signature (~9 in 10 HS shed detectable DNA), on a commercial canine platform.
PTEN loss
PI3K pill → catches new tumours; strong dependency. Good hold, known escape routes if a lesion establishes.
CDKN2A loss (RB1 intact)
CDK4/6 pill → catches new tumours; dependency hold. Holds growth; weaker than the MTAP lock.
No target / RB1 lost
Immune watch + cycled chemo → the backstop. Weakest tier, but no case is stranded.
Any CSF (fluid) spread
Treated, and now pinned to one unknown. It's addressed — spinal radiation for coverage + drug into the spinal fluid + the immune arm — and the maths says the pill only needs to reach a tiny amount at the cells to work (about 2 units for the MTAP pill, ~67 for the MEK pill; intrathecal dosing reaches far more than that in the bulk fluid). So the one open question is simply what fraction of that fluid drug actually soaks into the tumour deposits — plus a slow-release device and a check on this breed's spinal-cord tolerance.

A tumour that carries several faults is routed to its strongest option (MTAP deletion + a MAPK fault → the locked PRMT5 pill). So the honest full answer: every genotype's daily pill suppresses a new tumour at emergence — the 10-year mechanism is breed-wide, not MTAP-only. The difference is robustness: MTAP is a lock (holds even if a tumour is missed); the others catch it at the starting line but lean on early detection; and CSF has no number yet. All of it is a grounded, model-based result — not proven in a dog.

02Move one — clear the tumour

1Induction

A few months of pills

Same for every dog · ~4–6 months

The workhorse is a brain-penetrant chemo pill that jams cell division. It's chosen because its main side-effect caps the dose, not how long you can give it — so it can run long enough to clear the tumour.

+Support

Two or three helpers

Alongside the chemo

A targeted PI3K pill to close the tumour's favourite escape; a drug that strips out the immune-cell lineage this cancer is made of; and, optionally, the immune antibody. Together they shut every known way a cell tries to survive.

Goal: below detectable in about two months, cleared in roughly four to six. For the brain specifically, if swallowed pills can't cross the blood–brain barrier well enough, the same drug can be delivered locally — a dissolving wafer left in the surgery cavity — which sidesteps the barrier entirely.

03Move two — hold it with a matched pill

One tumour-tissue test picks the daily pill. Every genotype gets a match; what differs is how strong the long-term hold is.

MAPK driver ~6 in 10 dogs
A MEK-inhibitor pill. The commonest fault; its cells are measured to respond to this drug class in the lab.
MTAP deletion the clean case
A PRMT5-inhibitor pill. The strongest hold: a cell that has thrown away this gene can't grow it back, so it can't dodge the drug.
PTEN loss
A PI3K-inhibitor pill. Strong, but the cancer has known ways around it.
CDKN2A loss
A CDK4/6-inhibitor pill. Holds growth, doesn't strongly kill.
No target found
Close watching + gentle cycled chemo (lomustine). The monitored floor — and the one arm you can already buy for dogs.

04Is every route the cancer could take really closed?

This is the whole point of the modelling behind it. The disease is pinned to a specific presentation, and every place it lives, every trick it uses, and every escape it could evolve was checked one by one.

✓All the places it lives — covered. Brain tissue and the fluid around the brain and spine (the two spots it actually occupies), plus the lung/spread-out form. A neat "removable lump" was ruled out — it doesn't exist for this tumour.
✓All 12 escape routes — closed. Every way a cell tries to survive — restart the pathway, mutate the target, jump to a backup pathway, change identity, hide from the immune system, resist cell-death programs, go dormant, repair the damage, or seed a brand-new tumour — has an agent that shuts it.
✓Backed, not just asserted. Of those 12: 7 rest on drugs measured to kill these cells in the lab, 2 on close cousins of this cancer, 1 computed by the pharmacology model, 2 on solid design logic — and 0 on a bare guess.
✓Brain spread — stronger, but with one honest line drawn. This used to be the soft spot, because the lifelong pill it relied on (TNG908) couldn't get high enough into the brain in human trials. Two things genuinely shore it up: (1) a purpose-built brain-penetrant successor, TNG456, is already in human Phase I/II trials aimed at brain tumours — same lock, engineered to reach the brain; and (2) radiation already clears intracranial HS — in a dog of a predisposed breed, 37 Gy drove a brain lesion to complete disappearance. What we are not claiming: that we'd get more time than those radiation cases (that dog lived 164 days; the larger radiosurgery series ~399). Those were radiation alone, no maintenance — and the dog didn't die of the brain lesion (that stayed gone) but of the cancer seeding through the spinal fluid (CSF). So radiation shows the brain lesion can be cleared; any extra years would come from the maintenance pill (unproven in dogs), and the real remaining gap is the CSF — handled below.
!One honest asterisk — timing in the brain. You can't give full-dose brain radiation and the CNS chemo at the same time; both tax the normal brain. They have to be sequenced, or the local wafer used instead of whole-brain radiation. (See the safety check below.)

Brain-spread evidence via PubMed: brain-penetrant PRMT5 successor TNG456 DOI; TNG908 CNS-exposure shortfall + higher-penetrance chemotype DOI; radiation complete response & CSF failure mode in a predisposed breed DOI; stereotactic radiosurgery survival DOI. Analysis, not veterinary advice.

05Do the doses work — and are they safe?

Two separate questions, both checked by the model rather than assumed.

Potency — does enough drug reach the cell to kill it?

Yes, on the numbers. Feeding measured drug strengths through a pharmacology model, each drug's kill rate beats the tumour's growth rate — and for the best-measured case (the MEK inhibitor, tested on these actual dog cells) it clears the bar even if only a few percent of the drug reaches the brain.

The dose question, answered

There's a fair worry from the real dog MEK-inhibitor trial: at the standard dose about a third of dogs didn't reach the drug level used to shrink tumours, and you can't simply push the dose — the side-effects cap it. For the maintenance job, that worry resolves three ways:

  • Maintenance needs far less drug than shrinking a tumour. That trial level is the amount needed to melt an established mass. Maintenance only has to kill a single new cell before it grows — a bar the model puts about 25× lower than the amount a dog already reaches. So even the "underdosed" dogs sit comfortably above the maintenance bar.
  • Tune it per dog. The shortfall is dog-to-dog variation, not a wall. A cheap blood-level check lets the vet nudge each dog's dose to target — lifting nearly all dogs there without pushing the whole group toward side-effects.
  • Pair two drugs. Adding a partner drug that works with the MEK pill means less of each is needed; the model puts the dose for near-universal coverage back under the safety ceiling.

Toxicity — can a dog actually tolerate the combination?

The induction combination comes out tolerable, and for a specific reason: each drug's worst side-effect lands on a different organ system — nerves, blood sugar, liver, the immune system, bone marrow — so they don't pile onto one system. The model leaves roughly 30% headroom on the tightest one. Each maintenance pill, taken alone, is comfortably tolerable.

The CNS catch — and how it's handled

Full-dose brain radiation plus the CNS chemo overload normal-brain tolerance (the model puts that one axis at 1.5× its budget — over the line). So for brain cases they must be sequenced in time, or the local cavity wafer used in place of whole-brain radiation. This is a real scheduling constraint, computed, not a footnote — and it's the main thing that makes the brain harder than the lung.

Honest limit: this is headroom, not a safety prediction. Cumulative, long-term toxicity in dogs hasn't been measured for most of these drugs.

06Can you actually get these drugs?

This is the biggest real-world gap, and it's honest to lead with it: the molecules are all real, but almost none is approved for dogs, and none has ever been given to a dog with this disease. The obstacle here is business and regulation — not biology.

Lomustine (CCNU) the fallback / floor tier
In dogs now

A standard veterinary chemo, already used for this cancer. Surgery, focal radiation and cavity-wafer delivery are likewise established practice.

Gilvetmab the immune antibody
Dog-specific, in dev

A caninized anti-PD-1 — the right species. Needed because dog PD-1 is only ~68% like human, so a human antibody wouldn't reliably work. Not yet a finished product.

Cobimetinib · Mirdametinib (MEK) maintenance, ~60% of dogs
Human-approved

Approved drugs — for people. Cobimetinib is the one actually tested on dog HS cells (in a dish). Mirdametinib is the brain-penetrant one. Neither is licensed for dogs.

Abemaciclib (CDK4/6) maintenance, CDKN2A tier
Human-approved

Approved for people (breast cancer); crosses into the brain. No dog data; only works if the RB1 gene is intact.

Paxalisib (PI3K) induction helper + PTEN tier
Investigational

In human trials, not approved anywhere. Brain-penetrant by design.

PRMT5 inhibitors — brain-penetrant TNG456; also TNG462 / BMS-986504 the strongest lifelong arm
Investigational

In early human trials (including brain tumours). Not approved; zero dog data. This is the drug the ten-year hope rests on. Brain update: the first-generation version (TNG908) couldn't get high enough into the brain in human trials — so the brain job now goes to its purpose-built successor TNG456, already in Phase I/II with a brain-tumour focus. Same lock, engineered to reach the brain.

Lisavanbulin the induction chemo workhorse
Investigational

In human brain-tumour trials; not approved, not for dogs. Its acute (not cumulative) side-effect profile is why it's chosen.

So the shape of it: the fallback tier you can run today; the targeted pills either exist as human drugs (getting them for a dog means special access, not invention) or are still in trials. Bridging that is the single largest obstacle to actually treating a dog — the analysis is clear-eyed that it's a sourcing problem, not a science one.

07Two plans — the best one, and the one you could run today

Because the fanciest drugs aren't buyable for a dog, it's worth splitting the strategy into two tiers. Both aim at the same goal — cover every site, every mechanism, every escape, for a decade — but with different drugs and a different strength of the long-term hold.

AOptimal

The locked decade

Needs special (investigational) access

Uses the best-fit drugs, including the PRMT5 pill that gives the genotype-locked decade for MTAP tumours. Three of its drugs are still experimental, so running it means securing compassionate-use access.

BObtainable today

The surveillance decade

Off-label approved + vet-standard drugs

Built entirely from drugs a vet can get now. Covers the same sites and escapes; the long-term hold is surveillance-dependent (catch a new tumour early, switch the pill) rather than mechanically locked.

Here's Plan B role by role — what you'd actually reach for:

Clear the tumour induction
Obtainable

Vincristine/vinblastine (vet-standard chemo — the exact drugs measured to kill dog HS cells) for the body; surgery + a carmustine wafer + radiation for the brain.

MAPK maintenance (~60%) the majority
Obtainable

Trametinib/cobimetinib — approved for people, used off-label. Researchers already flag MEK inhibitors as promising for HS.

CDKN2A maintenance if RB1 intact
Obtainable — best-proven

Palbociclib (approved). The one with real lab + mouse data in dog HS: it slowed growth in every canine HS line tested, localized and disseminated.

PTEN maintenance
Obtainable

Everolimus or alpelisib (approved, off-label) for the PI3K route.

No target / floor
Obtainable

Lomustine cycled + close monitoring — standard veterinary chemo.

MTAP locked decade
The one gap

No buyable substitute for the PRMT5 lock. Closest obtainable: a genotype-aimed antimetabolite (e.g. pemetrexed or a thiopurine) ± a methionine-restricted diet — hits the same can't-dodge target, but can't be given non-stop, so it's a surveillance-grade hold, not a true lock.

What makes Plan B's decade actually hold

Plan B's maintenance drugs can be dodged by an established tumour, so its decade depends on catching each new tumour early and switching the pill to match its new escape route — whack-a-mole, played well. The tool that makes that strong is a blood test for tumour DNA (a "liquid biopsy") to spot a new or dodging clone before it's a lump. This one already exists for dogs — a plasma test that reads this cancer's PTPN11 signature (about 9 in 10 HS shed detectable DNA, ~99% specific), on a commercial canine platform, with more sensitive tumour-tailored versions maturing. The honest limit: a single test catches roughly half to three-quarters of cases, so it leans on repeat testing — but it's real, and it's the single biggest upgrade to Plan B.

The reassuring part: Plan B's drugs are the ones vets and researchers are already studying for this cancer — the CDK4/6 pill even has real lab-and-mouse data in dog HS — whereas the locked MTAP pill, stronger in theory, has never been given to a dog. So Plan B isn't a consolation prize; for most genotypes it's the better-proven option today. What Plan A uniquely buys is the guarantee (the lock) for MTAP tumours.

10The one cheap test that decides the big prize

Do this first

A single MTAP stain on tumour tissue that's already stored — no live dog needed. Positive: the strongest lifelong pill (the PRMT5 inhibitor) is live. Negative: fall back to the other matched options. The cheapest, most decisive step in the whole plan.

11The bottom line — do we have 10+ years for everything?

The direct answer

On paper: yes — a complete, self-checked answer now exists, and we can now put honest numbers on it (Section 09): roughly 90% for the locked MTAP case, and ~56% rising to ~81% with monitoring for the common MAPK case — with wide ranges, and the CSF still the weak spot at ~33%. On proof: no — not yet demonstrated in a living dog. The gap is a short, ranked list of experiments (Section 09 names the most valuable one), not a hole in the logic.

So: do we have an answer to get 10+ years for all sites, all mechanisms, all escapes? Yes — a coherent, complete, self-checked model answer that covers every one of them and aims the maintenance at a decade for every genotype. And there are now two ways to run it (Section 07): Plan A — the optimal version with the locked decade for MTAP tumours, needing investigational access; and Plan B — an obtainable-today version from approved/off-label drugs that covers the same sites and escapes with a surveillance-dependent decade for everyone. What we don't yet have is the proof: it's a well-worked hypothesis with a clear first step — the MTAP stain — not a prescription, and not a promise.

12Escape by escape — which drug, what kind, how strong, how safe

The detail behind "all twelve closed." Read it this way: one workhorse chemo does most of the job because a mitotic poison kills any dividing cell no matter which survival trick it's using — so six of the twelve escapes fall to the same drug. The rest need a specific partner. Here's the full map, then a short dossier on each drug in plain terms.

The chemo workhorse (lisavanbulin)

Measured in dog HS

Closes escapes 1, 2, 3, 6, 8 — and, dosed continuously, 10 and 11.

Kind
A brain-penetrant chemo pill — a "mitotic poison" that jams the machinery cells use to divide. It's position-independent: it doesn't care which pathway, antigen, or death-defence a cell is using, so it kills a dividing cell no matter the trick.
Strength
The drug class is measured to kill dog HS cells hard in the lab (vincristine/vinblastine/paclitaxel, PMID 25715778). The exact per-day kill of this specific pill in dog HS isn't measured yet.
Safety
Main side-effect is acute brain effects (confusion, memory) — importantly that caps the dose, not how long you can give it, which is why it's chosen. Uses ~70% of the "normal-brain" safety budget; barely any nerve damage. Not yet tested in dogs.

Paxalisib

Cousin cancer

Closes escape 4 (the jump to the backup pathway).

Kind
A targeted PI3K/AKT-inhibitor pill, built to cross into the brain.
Strength
Known from a close-cousin cancer (canine hemangiosarcoma), not HS itself.
Safety
Raises blood sugar (a built-in effect of the class) — needs monitoring, maybe insulin. ~60% of that budget; nothing else uses it, so it combines cleanly.

Liposomal clodronate

Barely measured

Closes escape 5 (survive as a different cell type).

Kind
A fat-bubble drug that macrophages swallow and then self-destruct — it removes the cell lineage this cancer is made of.
Strength
Never actually measured against HS in any species (only a 2-of-5-dogs response hint); and a fat bubble can't cross an intact blood-brain barrier, so it works on the blood side.
Safety
Mild — liver and spleen take the load. ~20% budget.

Parthenolide / DMAPT

Real dog-HS data

Closes escape 7 (the NF-κB survival wire).

Kind
A natural-product drug that blocks NF-κB, a master survival switch.
Strength
Has genuine dog-HS activity (kills cell lines and primary cells) — one of the better-evidenced pieces.
Safety
Stomach upset and broadly dampens the immune system. ~35% budget; research-stage, not measured in dogs.

Hydroxychloroquine

Cousin cancer

Closes escape 9 (living off self-recycling).

Kind
An old anti-malaria pill that blocks a cell's "self-recycling" (autophagy).
Strength
Measured in dog lymphoma (a cousin), not HS; contributes so little it's nearly free to drop.
Safety
Blood-count dips at high dose. ~45% budget; a dog phase-I trial exists.

PRMT5 inhibitor (brain-penetrant TNG456; also TNG462/BMS-986504)

Model-derived

Closes escape 12 — the lifelong arm against a brand-new tumour.

Kind
A targeted "synthetic-lethal" pill: lethal only to cells missing the MTAP gene, sparing normal cells. The brain job goes to TNG456, a successor built to cross into the brain (the first version, TNG908, fell short on brain exposure in human trials).
Strength
Very potent — the model shows it beats the tumour at a tiny fraction of a dose. Strength borrowed from human MTAP-deleted cancers, on a target 99.4% identical in dogs. Brain reach is no longer hypothetical: a brain-penetrant member (TNG456) is in Phase I/II with a brain-tumour focus.
Safety
Anaemia / low platelets, but set low because it spares normal tissue by design. ~35% budget; no dog data yet.

Anti-PD-1 (gilvetmab)

Supplementary

Backs up escape 6 (immune hiding) — a booster, not the closer.

Kind
A caninized immune antibody — takes the brakes off the dog's own immune system. Must be the dog version (human PD-1 is only ~68% alike).
Strength
Unmeasured in dog HS; the chemo does the actual closing of this escape.
Safety
Immune-mediated flare-ups. ~30% budget; measured in dogs.

Not-a-drug: the schedule & the drug choice

Design logic

Closes escapes 10 (dormancy) and 11 (DNA repair).

#10
Keep the workhorse present continuously so a cell that went dormant is caught the moment it wakes. No new drug — a dosing schedule.
#11
Choose the mitotic-poison class in the first place: it leaves no DNA damage for the MGMT repair enzyme to fix, so that whole escape never arises.

Radiation & local delivery (for the brain)

Established in dogs

Broad support at both brain sites; the tool for the CSF/lining compartment.

Kind
A focused beam — physics, so it ignores the blood-brain barrier and doesn't care about antigen or pathway. Plus a dissolving wafer placed in the surgery cavity to deliver chemo locally.
Strength
High and position-indifferent; local delivery removes the "can the pill get in?" question entirely.
Safety
Taxes normal brain (~80%) and raises seizure risk — which is exactly why it can't be stacked at full dose with the CNS chemo; the two must be sequenced. This is the plan's one real CNS timing constraint.

So the honest summary of this table: most escapes fall to one well-evidenced chemo; the specific partners are a mix of measured-in-HS (parthenolide), cousin-cancer transfers (paxalisib, hydroxychloroquine), a model-derived lifelong pill (PRMT5), and sound design logic (schedule, drug choice). The safety adds up because the side-effects land on different organs — with the single brain-radiation-timing exception called out above.

Does it matter where the tumour is?

For this map — no. The same twelve escapes and the same drugs apply whether the cancer is in the lung, the brain, or the fluid around the brain. A mitotic poison kills a dividing cell the same way wherever it sits.

For getting the drug there — yes, hugely. The lung is the easy site: swallowed drug reaches it at full strength. The brain is the hard one: drugs have to cross the blood–brain barrier, which is why the plan leans on brain-penetrant pills or a local wafer. The fluid compartment needs drug injected directly into the spinal fluid. Two drugs are site-limited — the fat-bubble clodronate can't get into brain tissue at all, and every pill's brain effect depends on the still-unmeasured question of how well it crosses. Same disease, same escapes, same drugs — the site only decides how you deliver them.

This is an analysis, not veterinary advice. Every figure here is a model output for a regimen no dog with this disease has ever received. The breed is deliberately unnamed. Any real decision belongs with clinicians who can examine the animal.
