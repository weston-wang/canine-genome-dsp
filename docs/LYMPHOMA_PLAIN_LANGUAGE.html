A modeling study · plain-language report

Can we cure lymphoma in dogs?

Standard chemotherapy puts almost every dog into remission — and almost every dog relapses. This is what a mathematical model of the disease says has to be true for a remission to last ten years, or for good.

The situation

The best chemotherapy is a brilliant pause, not a cure

Lymphoma is the most common blood cancer in dogs. The standard treatment, called CHOP, is a combination of four drugs. It is remarkably good at what it does — and remarkably consistent about what it doesn't do.

So the tumor shrinks, often completely, and then it returns. To make a remission last, you have to understand exactly why it comes back — and beat that specific thing. That is what this analysis set out to do: not to find another way to shrink the tumor, but to close every door it uses to escape.

How to read this report

Everything here comes from a computer model of how tumor cells grow, die, and evolve under treatment, calibrated against real published dog studies. The model's job is to make the logic explicit and testable. It is not medical advice, and none of the combinations described has been given to a dog on the strength of this model.

Why chemo can't finish the job

The bar: the speed a cure has to out-run

A tumor is a crowd of slightly different cells. Treatment sorts them.

Most of the cells are sensitive to the drugs and die. But a few carry a change that lets them survive — and those few keep growing. Each survivor type has a growth speed. The fastest one is the bar: any treatment meant to prevent relapse has to kill cells faster than the bar, or the cancer wins on time.

Here is the uncomfortable part. When you compare full chemotherapy to no drug at all, the bar barely moves:

Chemotherapy drives the sensitive cells sharply downward — that is the deep remission owners see. But the survivor cells keep growing at almost exactly the same speed whether the dog is on full chemo or nothing at all. Chemo moves the bar by about 2%. It decides how fast and how deeply the tumor shrinks, and almost nothing about whether it returns.

The cell that sets the bar is real

The fastest survivor is a cell that builds a molecular pump (called P-glycoprotein) which throws the two strongest chemo drugs back out before they can work. This isn't a guess — it's been measured in dog lymphoma cells, and blocking the pump restores the drugs' effect. It is the single most common reason lymphoma comes back. Piling on more of the same drugs cannot beat it: that cell is defined by pumping those exact drugs out.

The durability lever

Teach the immune system to do what the drugs can't

If chemo can't out-run the bar, what can? The answer is immunotherapy — in particular, engineered immune cells or antibodies that recognize a marker called CD20 on the surface of the cancer cells (this is the dog version of the CAR-T therapy now used in people).

Why does it help when chemo doesn't? Because the immune cells don't care about the drug pump. A cell can pump out chemotherapy all day long and still wear the CD20 flag the immune system was trained to hunt. Resistance to a drug doesn't change what the immune system sees. So a CD20 immune attack covers all three chemo-resistant cell types at once.

The model shows a sharp threshold. Below a certain strength, the immune attack does almost nothing extra. At or above it — enough to out-kill the bar — durability jumps:

~18%
still in remission long-term with chemo alone (model)
~24%
with a too-weak immune attack — barely better
~97%
at 10 years once the attack out-kills the bar (model)

It is all-or-nothing around the bar. A weak immune therapy is close to no immune therapy. A strong-enough one changes everything. That threshold is the whole game.

Every door the cancer can use

The escape routes — and how each one closes

A cure has to close all of them. Leave one open and the cancer walks through it.

1–3
The three chemo-resistant cells Closed
Drug-pump cells, a second pump, and cells that refuse to die. All three still wear CD20, so the immune attack sees them. (One honest caveat: the "refuse to die" cell is the hardest for the immune system to finish, so it's the least airtight of the three.)
4
Dropping the CD20 flag Real — closable
The cancer's counter-move to CD20 therapy is to stop making CD20 — and this really happens in treated dogs. The fix is a two-target therapy (CD19 and CD20 at once): losing one flag doesn't help if the other still gives it away. This exact two-target design has already been built for dogs.
5
Hiding in the brain Closed by immunotherapy
The brain is shielded by a barrier that blocks most chemo drugs — a hideout where cancer regrows untouched. In the model, when the drug can't get in, the brain becomes the main place relapse happens. Immune cells travel there on their own, so they close the hideout the drug can't reach. This is the sharpest single argument for immunotherapy over "just use more chemo."
6
The immune therapy doesn't take Measurable
Sometimes the engineered cells don't expand or get worn out. You can't always prevent it, but you can measure it early — a blood test for leftover cancer (MRD) tells you within weeks whether the therapy took, instead of waiting to see the cancer come back.
7
The cure itself is dangerous Reducible
The one treatment that already cures dogs — a bone-marrow transplant — also kills some of them, mostly from infection during recovery (around 7–13%). A durable remission in a dog that doesn't survive the treatment isn't a cure. Gentler versions of the procedure are a real lever on the final number.

Proof it's possible

Cure is not hypothetical — 40% of dogs already get there

The model's conclusion isn't a hope; it matches something that has already happened. The one real therapy with documented long-term cures in dog lymphoma is a bone-marrow transplant — and crucially, the version that cures most adds immune cells on top of the transplant, not just a bigger dose of radiation.

40%
of dogs cured (cancer-free 2+ years) with transplant + immune-cell therapy
~70%
relapse from transplant alone — the immune step is what carries it

This is exactly what the model predicts. A one-time blast of intensity (radiation, or chemo) sets up a deep remission but doesn't last — durability needs something that keeps working: a persistent immune attack. The real cure and the model agree on the shape of the answer.

The recipe for a lasting cure, in plain terms

Shrink the tumor hard and fast (chemotherapy). Then hand off to a strong-enough immune attack on CD20 that out-runs the bar and reaches the brain. Insure it with a second target so the cancer can't escape by dropping one flag. Watch for relapse with a blood test so you act early. And keep the transplant that consolidates it as safe as possible.

The question behind the question

Does two years cancer-free mean cured for good?

The real cure data counts a dog as "cured" if it's cancer-free at two years. But we want ten. So: if a dog makes it two years, is it home safe? The model answers by looking at when relapses happen — and the answer depends entirely on how strong the treatment was.

✓
A strong-enough treatment 2 years ≈ safe
When the immune attack clearly beats the bar, relapses essentially stop after the first stretch. A dog cancer-free at two years is cancer-free at ten — the two numbers become the same. Clearing the bar with room to spare is what makes the two-year mark trustworthy.
~
A treatment that just barely clears the bar A small late tail
About 3 in 100 dogs cancer-free at two years still relapse sometime over the next eight — and it's spread across the whole decade. Almost all of it is a rare, slow-growing drug-pump escape (not the CD20-flag escape), which is why the two-target fix doesn't help here — only a stronger attack does. This is exactly what the leftover-cancer blood test is for: catching that rare late return early.
✕
A weak treatment (or chemo alone) 2 years is misleading only in reverse
Here almost everyone who relapses does so in the first several months — so two years cancer-free does reliably predict ten. The catch is that hardly any dogs make it to two years cancer-free in the first place.

Bottom line: two years cancer-free is a trustworthy sign of a real, lasting cure — if the treatment beat the bar with margin. And since dogs live about 10–13 years, ten years cancer-free is, for most dogs, a cure for life. (One honest caveat the model can't see: over a decade an older dog faces other risks — a second, unrelated cancer, or old age itself — so surviving the lymphoma is necessary but not the whole story.)

What the cure costs

Is it worth the risk? Weighing power against harm

A treatment that's powerful but too toxic to give isn't a cure. Each piece of the plan has a different balance of power and harm — and, encouragingly, they don't all pull the same way.

1
The immune attack (CAR-T) Toxicity is separable
Its main side effect is an immune over-reaction (fever, inflammation) that doctors already control with specific drugs — without weakening the cancer-killing. Power and harm are on separate dials. The first CAR-T given to a dog was well tolerated.
2
Chemotherapy A real trade-off
On its own you can lower the dose freely, since it was never the thing providing the cure. But as part of the full plan, a deeper knock-down early makes the immune attack's job easier — and chemo's own toxicity (low blood counts, gut upset) limits how deep you can safely push. This is the one genuine power-vs-harm tension in the plan.
3
The transplant The real price
This is where toxicity bites hardest: roughly 7–13% of dogs don't survive the procedure itself, mostly from infection during recovery. Gentler versions of the transplant are an active way to make the cure safer.

Searching for the combination

We didn't guess the recipe — we searched for it

Listing escape routes in prose lets you quietly claim one is "handled." So instead we built a model where each treatment's coverage is computed from how it works, then had a computer try every combination and report which ones close all eleven escape routes at once.

A treatment fails an escape route in exactly three ways, and the model works each one out rather than taking our word for it:

  • It's blocked in the wrong place. Cutting a signalling wire only helps above the cut — a fault below it routes around you.
  • Its target disappeared. A therapy aimed at the CD20 flag dies with that flag. A two-flag therapy survives losing one.
  • It gets pumped out. The drug-pump escape defeats exactly the drugs it pumps — and, importantly, not the steroid.

Three requirements survive all of that, and together they rule out every conventional chemotherapy drug: the treatment must keep working on cells that have stopped dividing, must not depend on a single flag, and must not be pumped out. What's left is a short list — the steroid, hydroxychloroquine, venetoclax, and cell-based immune therapy.

What the search found

✓
Body-wide B-cell lymphoma Closes comfortably
Chemotherapy plus a steroid, CD20 cell therapy and hydroxychloroquine closes every route with roughly 3.5× the killing power needed — the widest safety margin anywhere in the analysis.
✓
Body-wide T-cell lymphoma Closes — with a different drug
Here venetoclax earns its place. Real lab data shows dog T-cell lymphoma is highly sensitive to it while most B-cell lymphoma is resistant — so the two types genuinely need different recipes, not the same one at different doses.
✓
B-cell lymphoma hiding in the brain Closes, but only just
Needs a change of delivery, not a bigger dose: chemotherapy injected directly into the spinal fluid, plus radiation, steroid, cell therapy and hydroxychloroquine. Margin is thin — about 1.3× what's needed.
✕
T-cell lymphoma hiding in the brain Nothing available closes it
The one genuine dead end. Everything available today, combined, still falls about 16% short. The reason is precise: beating the "stopped-dividing" cells in the brain needs a cell therapy that can travel there, and the B-cell version exists while the T-cell version does not. Build that one therapy and this closes too.

Catching it early

What early detection really buys you

Finding cancer early is usually described as "starting sooner." The model says the real prize is bigger and different. Escape routes appear by chance as cells divide, so the more tumour cells there are, the more likely any given escape has already happened.

At a lump you can feel, essentially all eleven escapes are already present. Caught early, only about seven are. At the very earliest detectable level, almost none have appeared yet. Early detection doesn't just start the clock sooner — it reduces the number of problems you have to solve.

But two escapes never go away

The escapes that come from a mutation thin out when you catch things early. The two that come from cells simply changing behaviour — going dormant, or exhausting the immune system — are still essentially guaranteed no matter how early you look. So a treatment that works on non-dividing cells stays mandatory at every stage. Early detection shrinks the problem; it doesn't change its shape.

And it does not help with the brain hideout: the escapes that matter there are present whether you catch the disease early or late. Finding it sooner doesn't get a drug past a barrier it can't cross.

Not all lymphoma is the same

B-cell vs. T-cell: one is a much harder problem

Lymphoma comes in two main flavors, and the plan above is really a plan for the common one.

B-cell (the common ~70%)

Grows more slowly, so the bar is lower — and it wears CD20, so the whole immunotherapy plan applies directly. This is where the model reaches ~97% ten-year durability once the immune attack clears the bar.

T-cell (harder — but not only harder)

Grows faster, so the bar is higher, and it doesn't wear CD20 at all, so the whole CD20 route is unavailable. But it has a real advantage B-cell lacks: it is highly sensitive to venetoclax, a drug that kills without needing cells to divide.

So the two aren't the same problem at different difficulty settings — they need genuinely different recipes. B-cell disease has the immune route; T-cell disease has the apoptosis route. The one place T-cell truly runs out of options is the brain, where it needs a travelling cell therapy that nobody has built yet.

What's solid and what isn't

Where this is grounded, and where it's a sketch

Real, and measured in dogs

  • The drug-pump resistance that sets the bar, and that it spares the steroid in the regimen.
  • Chemotherapy's 98% response rate and ~6-month typical remission.
  • CD20 loss happening in real dogs treated with CD20 therapy — and the two-target fix.
  • The 40% cure rate with transplant plus immune cells, and its risks.
  • Blood tests that detect leftover cancer before it's visible.

Illustrative — the model's assumptions, not measurements

  • The exact growth speeds, kill strengths, and the precise height of the bar.
  • How strong a real CD20 immune therapy actually is — never yet measured in a finished dog trial.
  • How completely the brain barrier blocks each chemo drug in dogs.

The value of the model isn't the exact numbers — it's the logic: which levers matter, which don't, and what a real trial should measure next.

What would settle the open questions

  • Measure the immune therapy's real strength — does a dog CD20 CAR-T actually clear the bar?
  • Identify the escape route at each relapse — pump, death-dodging, or flag-dropping — because each needs a different counter.
  • Follow transplanted dogs to 5 and 10 years, not just 2, since a decade is the real goal.
