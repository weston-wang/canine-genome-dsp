Canine multicentric lymphoma · model report

All four cases close, using only treatments a vet can legally obtain

Two treatments I had relied on turned out to be unavailable by law. Rebuilt without them, every escape route still closes in the model: about six months of a licensed canine tablet for the body, and for the brain one radiation course plus methotrexate into the spinal fluid every three weeks. The remaining risk is no longer the drugs — it is the anaesthetics and spinal taps.

"Closes" means that after counting side effects for the whole combination, the treatments kill the cells of every one of 23 escape routes faster than they regrow, and clear them inside the time the treatments are given. The brain figures use the pessimistic end of the drug-strength range and a three-week dosing interval. Nothing here has been tried in a dog.

The legal check, and what it removed

A vet may use a human medicine in a dog, but the rule is narrower than it sounds: in the US it covers drugs approved by the FDA, whether for animals or for people, used by a vet who has examined the animal. It does not make an unapproved drug legal. Two things I had been counting on fail that test, and both were carrying a closure:

  • The oral cytarabine prodrug is approved only in Japan. There is no legal route to it for a dog in the US. It was the basis of my earlier "2.7 to 3.4 years of oral cytarabine" plan for the B-cell brain.
  • There is no licensed anti-CD20 antibody for dogs. The one that existed was withdrawn from sale in 2017; the current one is experimental. It was the basis of the B-cell body closing on day 74.

Everything in the plans above is either a licensed canine drug, an FDA-approved human drug used off-label, or a procedure:

  • Verdinexor — a licensed canine lymphoma tablet (fully approved January 2026). Its label is twice-weekly dosing until the disease progresses, so the six months the plans need is ordinary labelled use. This is the firmest ground in the whole project.
  • Methotrexate into the spinal fluid — the preservative-free product is FDA-approved and the spinal-fluid route is on its human label. Giving it with cytarabine this way is published in 112 dogs.
  • Cytarabine, prednisolone, hydroxychloroquine, romidepsin — approved, used off-label. Romidepsin and hydroxychloroquine turn out not to matter: dropping them changes nothing.
  • Matched-donor transplant and radiation — procedures, not drug approvals. The transplant is essential in every plan and needs a perfectly matched littermate, about a 25% chance per sibling.

Why the brain result is no longer fragile

It was fragile, and that was a fair criticism. If methotrexate is the only thing covering the brain and it reaches only half of it, nothing closes, even over ten years — the cliff is sharp.

What fixed it was finding that one of the model's own numbers was wrong. I had verdinexor reaching the brain at 5% of blood level, a generic default with no source behind it. Its sister drug, nearly the same molecule, has been measured at 60 to 72% of blood level in the brains of mice, rats and monkeys with an intact blood–brain barrier. At 30%, below that measurement, half-strength methotrexate closes both brains. So there are now two independent routes, resting on measurements of two different drugs, covering different parts of the brain: methotrexate the fluid spaces, verdinexor the brain tissue itself, and the radiation course both, once.

Three other worries shrank when I checked them properly:

  • The radiation dose is mild for a dog, not aggressive — about 37 to 40% of the late-effect dose in the protocol dogs routinely receive for brain tumours, and no severe late damage was reported across four canine series totalling more than 300 dogs.
  • The white-matter damage figure I quoted was overstated. The 29% was mostly findings on scans without symptoms, appearing at three months, and in that study the radiation was not the risk factor — the spinal-fluid methotrexate was. Two groups given exactly this radiation dose had stable thinking and memory tests out to three years.
  • The drug-strength number I was least sure of does not matter. A dose into the spinal fluid reaches a concentration thousands of times higher than the level needed to kill the cells, so what counts is how long it stays up, not the exact killing concentration. Varying that concentration across its whole plausible range changes the answer by only about 1.6 times, and the plans above use the pessimistic end.

In children, this is established practice rather than theory: brain irradiation was dropped from leukemia treatment entirely in favour of 13 to 27 spinal-fluid doses, and fewer than 2% relapsed in the brain.

The realistic programs, without waiting for a new product

  • B-cell body (closed): anti-CD20 antibody, verdinexor, hydroxychloroquine, a transplant from a perfectly matched littermate (with its body radiation), and oral cytarabine. Every escape route in the body is cleared by day 74.
  • B-cell brain: the body program plus one 23.4 Gy whole-brain (or brain-and-spine) radiation course plus methotrexate injected into the spinal fluid about every 2 to 3 weeks for about 13 months (28 to 36 doses). If the dormant cells are as resistant to radiation as the most resistant dog lymphoma line it takes about 28 months; with no radiation about 45 months. An alternative with no injections is oral cytarabine and verdinexor for 2.7 to 3.4 years. Radiation matters because it kills resting lymphoid cells (non-dividing leukemia cells die from it in about 85% of patients) and no cell pump can remove it. Methotrexate in the spinal fluid matters because it reaches a level 100,000 times what kills dog lymphoma cells in a dish, so pumps cannot keep it out, and the dormant cells are killed as they slowly wake up.
  • T-cell body: the T-cell plan (prednisolone, hydroxychloroquine, verdinexor, romidepsin, matched transplant, oral cytarabine) with verdinexor continued about 6 months. Verdinexor is licensed for continuous dosing; the longest use documented in a dog is 17 months.
  • T-cell brain: the T-cell plan, plus the same radiation course, plus methotrexate into the spinal fluid for about 13 months. The matched transplant is essential (without it the methotrexate would be needed for more than 11 years). Oral cytarabine does little here (T-cell lymphoma cells are less sensitive and dCK loss defeats it), so methotrexate carries it. If dog T-cell lymphoma cells are very resistant to radiation it takes about 28 months; with no radiation about 45 months. Verdinexor does reach the brain in monkeys, rats and mice (60 to 72% of blood level for its sister drug), but it is not needed.

What is actually left. The biggest remaining risk is not a drug — it is the procedures. Each radiation session and each spinal injection needs general anaesthesia, and repeated access to the spinal fluid in dogs has real complications: in the published port studies two dogs died of infection or fluid build-up and catheters blocked or moved, and in a series of 19 dogs given four injections through the skin there were five seizures and one fatal brain haemorrhage. Together with 13 anaesthetics that is a few percent risk of dying from the treatment itself, and no part of my model accounts for it. It is the reason to prefer the 12-dose schedule, and to test whether a single larger radiation session could replace 13 smaller ones. Still genuinely unknown: nobody has measured how much verdinexor reaches a dog's brain, or the methotrexate level in a dog's spinal fluid on this schedule. Those are the two cheapest measurements that would settle this, and neither needs a new product — just a tissue sample from a dog already on a licensed drug.

Is anyone building the treatments for dogs?

I searched institution trial pages, grants, company pipelines and papers (veterinary trials are not on the usual US registry). Only one of the near-future treatments has a dog program:

  • CAR-T for B-cell lymphoma: yes. Penn has an active trial of autologous CAR-T cells (seven dogs so far; the cells did not multiply or last, and a fix is shown in the lab and in mice). A company's off-the-shelf version is on hold. A tandem CD19/CD20 version is lab-stage, with a stated plan for a dog trial.
  • CD3 T-cell engager: not found. A dog CD3 antibody exists and a grant proposed a related idea for T-cell cancers, but no dog engager has been built. A dog engager of a different kind (it recruits NK cells) is in dogs for sarcoma.
  • CD7 or CD5 CAR-T for T-cell lymphoma: not found. Dog CD5 antibodies exist, but CD5 is often missing on the tumour cells.
  • CAR-T into the spinal fluid, or made inside the patient, in dogs: not found. Both are real in humans and monkeys.
  • How long licensing takes for dogs: 3 to 5.4 years for recent dog cancer drugs that were already in dogs; likely 7 to 10 years for something not yet built.

So the claim that these are "near-future for dogs" holds only for the B-cell CAR-T. For the others, what exists is human trial evidence for that kind of treatment. None of them is needed for the plans above. Two other things are being worked on in dogs now: a radioactive drug (yttrium-90 NM600) for T-cell lymphoma, which does not look like it reaches the brain (dogs given it developed brain metastases), and verdinexor in dogs with brain tumours (Colorado State).

The number the closure turns on

The brain and the dormant cells are covered by CAR-T cells (and, for B-cell, the engager). The B-cell plan without the engager clears once the CAR-T cells kill at 0.08 per day or more, and survives any one treatment dropping and every treatment halving from 0.2 per day. The T-cell plan clears from 0.12 per day and is robust from 0.2 per day. The central value carried over from human data is 0.35 per day. In dogs, CAR-T cells made so far (seven dogs) did not expand or last, so this is the live number. Two near-future routes address it: CAR-T cells made inside the patient rather than in a lab (first human results 2025 to 2026), and, for B-cell lymphoma, the engager, which does not depend on CAR-T cells at all.

The growth rate the treatments must beat

Every result above is measured against how fast the resistant cells regrow: 0.09 per day (doubling in about 8 days). That number started as an illustrative placeholder, which is the kind of input that must not be left unchecked. It is now worked out from dog data: after relapse and in untreated dogs, regrowth works out to between 0.015 and 0.12 per day, and the dog cells' maximum possible rate (no cell loss at all) is 0.20 per day. The 0.09 sits at the high end of what was observed, so it is a cautious choice, but not a ceiling. Raising it to 0.20 with every treatment held at the same strength: the B-cell plan still closes at both the cautious and the central numbers; the T-cell plan still closes at the central numbers, and at the cautious numbers only if regrowth is no faster than about 0.10 per day.

Items that were outside the check, now checked

  • Loss of immune markers (MHC): in people it is a mismatched-donor problem (29% of relapses after half-matched donors, 7% after unrelated adult donors). With a perfectly matched littermate there is nothing to lose. Antibody, CAR-T and engager do not need the markers. Condition: a matched donor.
  • Drug resistance with no traced mechanism: in people, CAR-T gives 31% lasting responses at about 5 years in lymphoma that resisted chemotherapy, so it does not defeat the immune treatments in the plans.
  • Eye: most dogs with eye lymphoma had it alone and did not progress after the eye was removed; treat locally (eye radiation or removal) if it appears. Testis: radiation of the other testis prevented testis relapse in people, and neutered dogs have none.
  • Brain tissue reach: measured only as fluid and blood ratios; nobody has measured drug levels in the brain lymphoma that has not broken the barrier. The plans use the measured ratios and clear with several treatments covering each brain route.
  • Later risks (second cancers about 9 to 21% at 10 years in people after transplant or CAR-T, treatment deaths about 18% at 10 years after CAR-T) are costs of treatment, not escape routes, so they are not part of this check.

A correction

An earlier version of this report gave the oral cytarabine level in the spinal fluid as 1.0 to 3.6 µM, measured. That was a ratio measured at the low point of the day multiplied by a peak blood level. The measured spinal-fluid levels were 0.04 to 0.27 µM. The model now uses 0.3 µM (the low end of the worked-out daily average). That makes the T-cell plan depend on the cytarabine into the spinal fluid at the cautious numbers; the B-cell plan is unchanged. An earlier statement that giving existing drugs for longer cannot close the brain had only been tested to 2 years; it is withdrawn.

How each class was handled

  • Stem-cell transplant: credited from dog data (8 of 9 first-remission dogs alive past 4 years). It is in both plans.
  • Vaccines: credited from dog survival data, small and needing dividing cells; they are not needed for closure.
  • Inhibitors: credited from human lab and drug-level data; the useful ones are in the plans (verdinexor, hydroxychloroquine). None reaches the dormant cells in the brain. A search for new ones that do found only candidates without a derivable kill rate (CDK9 inhibitors) or an ungraded estimate (artesunate). Thiotepa was credited from human outcome data; it ends with its treatment window and so does not close the brain alone.
  • T-cell engagers: credited from human data; dog version to build (and given gradually, because a dog went into a cytokine storm on a T-cell activating antibody). Armed T cells: no lymphoma data in any species, no credit. eBAT toxin: its targets are low in dog lymphoma, so it is set aside.

Conditions, and where each one stands

  • The treatments under development are built for dogs. B-cell CAR-T: a program is active but the cells have not yet multiplied in dogs. Dog engager, CD7/CD5 CAR-T and the spinal-fluid route: no program found.
  • For the B-cell brain without building anything: oral cytarabine (a Japanese human drug, research import for dogs) for about 4 years, reaching the spinal fluid on average at about 0.1 µM or more. Status: not shown. Measured spinal-fluid levels in four healthy dogs were 0.04 to 0.27 µM at the low point of the day; the daily average was worked out as 0.3 to 1.4 µM but never measured. Years of dosing is not documented: people take it 10 to 14 days in 28, and in dogs the longest daily course was 7 days.
  • CAR-T kills at 0.2 per day or more in dogs (0.12 for the T-cell plan to close at all; the B-cell plan using only treatments with a dog program needs 0.12). Status: not shown in dogs.
  • A perfectly matched donor exists. Status: depends on the dog (25% per littermate).
  • For the T-cell plan at the cautious CAR-T numbers, regrowth is no faster than about 0.10 per day. Status: the dog data put it at 0.015 to 0.12 per day.
  • If an engager is used, it is given gradually. Status: standard practice in people.
  • Eye disease, if it appears, is treated locally.

This is a model result: the numbers are measured in dogs, worked out from measurements, calibrated from outcomes, or carried over from human data with the reasoning written down. It is not a demonstration in a dog.
