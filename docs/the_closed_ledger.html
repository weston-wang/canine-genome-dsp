Canine histiocytic sarcoma · primary intracranial

The Closed Ledger

Every enumerated escape route, at every site the disease occupies, closed by a named agent at a stated evidence grade. The answer is a checklist, not a probability.

All 16 escape routes close at both occupied sites — and all 16 also close using only drugs you can obtain today. Five of eight conditions hold, and none of the three outstanding is an engineering problem.

And every route closes with drugs you can obtain today. Getting a drug past an intact blood–brain barrier was the binding constraint at every stage of this work, and it is now settled by measurement rather than inference: ribociclib, licensed, reaches 65–634 nM unbound in gadolinium-non-enhancing brain tumour — tissue behind an intact barrier by definition — against a 40 nM target. Checked route by route against licensed drugs only, all 16 close: 3 by a computed kill margin, 12 structurally, 1 gated on a stain, none open. The weaker part of that is stated below, not buried: structural closure means "the lesion cannot apply to these drugs", which is a smaller claim than out-killing it.

Closed within a stated catalogue: 25 therapy-modality classes (15 in the model, 10 excluded on a stated scientific ground, none unassessed, none excluded for lacking canine data) and 16 escape routes derived independently of the list already in hand.

The case

Primary intracranial, non-disseminated histiocytic sarcoma in a predisposed breed. An extra-axial mass on the meninges that invades brain tissue in 23 of 23 dogs and seeds the leptomeninges in 19 of 19. Extracranial spread of primary CNS disease has never been reported, so each site is a bounded target.

Why ten years needs two moves

Killing this tumour harder cannot reach a decade, because the breed is born predisposed: clearing one tumour leaves the inherited fault untouched. The unlock is that the predisposition and the tumour are the same lesion — a deletion on canine chromosome 11 removing CDKN2A/B and MTAP, measured in 62.8% of cases.

  1. Clear the tumour that is there.
  2. Hold a matched oral drug indefinitely, so the next tumour never establishes.

Access was the obstacle, and it is not a device problem

A generic small molecule given systemically reaches 2.1% of invaded brain tissue and 0.5% of CSF. At those numbers every route is open, at −0.049 and −0.036 per day. That is also why radiation alone fails: a 21-day course against a year is a 5.8% duty cycle.

The requirement is not to bypass the barrier. It is access 0.196 in brain tissue and 0.0145 in CSF, and measured intrinsic penetration clears it.

Convection-enhanced delivery has the best canine evidence of any device and fails on duty cycle at one infusion a year. Intrathecal injection clears CSF and is obtainable today, so it is the backup rather than a requirement.

The one open site, and how it closed

Until now the ledger had a soft spot. The bulk of this tumour sits outside the brain proper, on the leaky side of the barrier, where reaching it is easy. Its invading edge sits behind a barrier that is still intact, and every candidate for that edge was either a compound no vet can obtain or an inference — a rodent ratio, a generic figure, or a concentration measured in a lesion whose barrier was already broken.

The question had been asked in the wrong units. “Does a drug get into brain” and “does a drug get into tumour that still has an intact barrier around it” are different questions. And there is a direct way to answer the second: tumour that does not light up on contrast MRI is, by definition, tumour behind an intact barrier. Give a drug, remove the tumour, and measure the drug in the part that did not light up.

Two human trials did exactly that for ribociclib, a licensed CDK4/6 inhibitor.

Every reported value clears the bar, including the single lowest patient. Four things make this the strongest input in the project:

  • It is a measurement in the exact compartment at issue, not a transfer across compartments.
  • The genotype matches. The trial enrolled patients selected for CDKN2A/B deletion with wild-type Rb — the same lesion 62.8% of these dogs carry — and CDK4/6 dependency is already measured in canine histiocytic cells.
  • The drug was shown to be working there: Rb phosphorylation and Ki-67 both fell in the same tissue.
  • It is licensed, oral and daily, so penetration and continuous presence come from one schedule. No catheter, no implant.

The same trial supplies its own control. A second drug given alongside — everolimus — was undetectable in the same tumours. The method can return a negative, and did.

The number that proves the method works

The same group measured ceritinib in the same compartment with the same assay and got 6 nM — and said so: "insufficient for target modulation." A method that returns a clean negative for one drug is a method whose positive for another means something.

Two routes that had no licensed answer, and now do

Cells that are not dividing

Every licensed drug that reaches the invading edge acts on a dividing cell — the CDK4/6 inhibitors, radiation, the PARP and Wee1 inhibitors, all of them. A dormant survivor simply waits them out.

Dordaviprone does not work that way. It forces a protease inside the mitochondrion to chew up the proteins of the cell's energy machinery, so it kills by collapsing metabolism — and a dormant cell needs energy just as much as a dividing one. It received FDA accelerated approval in 2025, it is an oral once-weekly tablet, and its approved disease is itself the access evidence: diffuse midline glioma is unresectable, infiltrative and largely non-enhancing, so working there is working behind an intact barrier.

Does its target exist in a dog? Computed from the real sequences rather than assumed. ClpP is 92.65% identical overall — and that figure understates it, because 10 of the 20 differences sit in a 56-residue tag that is cut off as the protein enters the mitochondrion, and 7 more in a floppy tail. The working core is 98.41% identical, and both catalytic residues are identical.

Graded as a structural closure, not a computed one: there is no canine data for this drug and no published tumour concentration, so no kill rate is claimed — and once-weekly dosing sits awkwardly against the requirement for a drug always to be present.

The inherited deletion itself

This gap was a mistake in how the question had been framed, not a missing drug. It had been treated as needing an MTAP-directed drug, and those are all still in trials. But the deletion removes two genes, and the second one is CDKN2A — whose protein product has exactly one job: holding CDK4/6 in check. So a CDK4/6 inhibitor does the job of the gene that was deleted. It is matched to the inherited fault rather than to a passing mutation, which is what makes it an anchor: a second tumour from the same deletion is met by the same drug. And it is licensed.

What is honestly weaker here

12 of the 16 routes close structurally. "This drug class cannot be escaped that way" is sound reasoning, but it is not the same as out-killing the tumour. The full regimen gets +0.59 per day from a derived kill rate; the licensed-only regimen has no equivalent cytotoxic.

What remains — three measurements and supplies, not three mechanisms

  • A computed kill margin at the invading edge. The access is measured and the mechanism is covered, but no licensed drug delivers a derived kill rate at that site.
  • A measured brain concentration on the PI3K route, where the evidence is worse than silence. Ribociclib alone had limited effect in human glioblastoma despite working on its target, and the tumour escaped through PI3K. The drug meant to block that escape could not be detected in the tumour at all. This is the weakest row in the table, and it is named rather than averaged away.
  • An anchor that survives loss of the Rb gene. CDK4/6 inhibition needs Rb intact. Other drugs still cover that route — dordaviprone's kill does not care about Rb — but nothing licensed stands guard on the inherited fault once Rb is gone. That is what the MTAP drug would do, and it is still in trials.

The limits

  • No agent in any regimen here has been given to a dog with this disease. Every margin is a model output from graded inputs.
  • The honest comparator is a 44-day median for intracranial disease under surgery, radiation and chemotherapy, and a 568-day median with a 243-day disease-free interval for localized disease after debulking plus lomustine. The distance from there to ten years is the claim, and no measurement here closes it.
  • Brain-penetrant is not brain-effective, and ribociclib is the case in point. It reached its target in brain tumour and measurably suppressed it, and human glioblastoma still progressed in about ten weeks — by rerouting through PI3K/mTOR. Reaching the cell is necessary, not sufficient. Sagopilone makes the point harder still: an access exemplar for the microtubule class, it produced no objective responses in glioblastoma at all.
  • Three agents in the full regimen cannot be dispensed to a dog today. None has to be discovered — two are in human trials. The licensed-only regimen closes every route, but leans on structural closure for 12 of the 16, which is a weaker form of the same claim.
  • Two of the newest pieces have no canine data at all. Dordaviprone's kill is a class-mechanism transfer with a computed target match and no dog ever dosed for this disease; its once-weekly schedule is in tension with the requirement for continuous presence.
  • The cheapest decisive test has never been run. One MTAP immunostain on archived tissue. If the tumour is MTAP-intact, the strongest tier does not apply.
  • The largest remaining uncertainty is not a drug. How often a cleared, predisposed dog throws a new primary drives most of the spread in every scenario. That is an observational cohort, not an experiment.

What to do first

  1. MTAP immunostain, with p16 and Rb, on archived tumour tissue. Decides C5 and which tier applies.
  2. Run the ten existing canine HS cell lines against a colchicine-site tubulin binder and against a PRMT5 inhibitor. Converts two transfers into measurements, with no new animals.
  3. A canine Cmax for the induction agent. Turns the last cross-species transfer into a dog number.
  4. An observational cohort of cleared, predisposed dogs, to measure the second-primary rate.
